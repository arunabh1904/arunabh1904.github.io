## 2019 – BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding

**Paper:** [arXiv:1810.04805](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805) · Preprint 2018, NAACL 2019 · [Reference code](https://github.com/google-research/bert)

## Summary

> BERT pretrains a Transformer encoder to recover selected tokens using context on both sides, then fine-tunes the full encoder for downstream tasks. The key change is the information available at every layer, not simply a larger classifier. Its original ablations separate bidirectionality from next-sentence prediction and show that the masking mixture matters differently for frozen features and full fine-tuning. The reusable component is a pretrained initialization: each downstream task still receives its own fine-tuned model.

## Core Insights

### Hide the prediction target so both directions can contribute

If a bidirectional network could see the token it was asked to predict, the training objective would permit trivial copying. BERT selects some positions as prediction targets and corrupts their inputs. The encoder can then use visible evidence to the left and right without being directly handed most target identities.

This differs from concatenating separately trained forward and backward representations. In BERT, the contextual computation at every layer can jointly combine both directions. A word in an answer span can therefore incorporate a later disambiguating phrase before the span classifier scores it.

The source transfer diagram shows the other half of the recipe. Pretraining learns masked-token and sentence-pair objectives; downstream training changes the output task while updating the same encoder parameters. The models start from a shared checkpoint but are fine-tuned separately.

![BERT source Figure 1 showing pretraining and downstream fine-tuning with a shared encoder architecture](/assets/images/bert-pre-training-of-deep-bidirectional-transformers-for-language-understanding-paper-figure.png)
*Fig 1: The encoder architecture transfers from pretraining to downstream tasks, while output heads change. Fine-tuning updates all parameters; the diagram does not imply one frozen model serves every task unchanged. | source: [BERT, Figure 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)*

### Selection, corruption, and supervision are three different masks

BERT selects 15% of eligible WordPiece positions for prediction. Among those selected positions, 80% become `[MASK]`, 10% become a random token, and 10% remain unchanged. Every selected position retains its original token as the label, including those left unchanged or replaced by something else. Unselected positions contribute no masked-LM loss.

For 100 eligible tokens, the expected counts are 12 mask replacements, 1.5 random replacements, and 1.5 unchanged targets. These are expectations over sampling, not guaranteed counts in each short sentence. Corrupting 15% of words and computing a reconstruction loss at every position would train a different objective.

One corruption draw per selected position keeps the alternatives mutually exclusive. This PyTorch example isolates that bookkeeping; it is not the complete original data generator, which also constructs paired spans and training examples.

```python
import torch


def mask_tokens(
    ids: torch.Tensor,
    special_mask: torch.Tensor,
    mask_token_id: int,
    vocab_size: int,
    probability: float = 0.15,
) -> tuple[torch.Tensor, torch.Tensor]:
    """Return corrupted ids and original-token labels; -100 ignores a position."""
    if ids.dtype != torch.long or ids.ndim not in (1, 2):
        raise ValueError("ids must be a long tensor of shape [T] or [B, T]")
    if special_mask.shape != ids.shape or special_mask.dtype != torch.bool:
        raise ValueError("special_mask must be boolean with the same shape as ids")
    if special_mask.device != ids.device:
        raise ValueError("ids and special_mask must be on the same device")
    if not 0 <= probability <= 1 or not 0 <= mask_token_id < vocab_size:
        raise ValueError("invalid selection probability or mask token id")

    selected = (torch.rand(ids.shape, device=ids.device) < probability) & ~special_mask
    labels = ids.masked_fill(~selected, -100)
    corrupted = ids.clone()  # Do not overwrite the caller's original tokens.
    choice = torch.rand(ids.shape, device=ids.device)
    replace_mask = selected & (choice < 0.8)
    replace_random = selected & (choice >= 0.8) & (choice < 0.9)
    random_ids = torch.randint(vocab_size, ids.shape, device=ids.device)
    corrupted[replace_mask] = mask_token_id
    corrupted[replace_random] = random_ids[replace_random]
    return corrupted, labels
```

The remaining selected positions keep their original inputs but remain supervised. A small toy batch can select no positions; skip its masked-LM mean loss rather than averaging an empty target set. ### Segment identity does not reset token position

A sentence pair is packed as `[CLS] A [SEP] B [SEP]`. “Sentence” here can mean a contiguous text span rather than exactly one grammatical sentence. Each position receives the sum of a token embedding, a segment embedding, and a learned position embedding.

![BERT source Figure 2 showing token, segment, and position embeddings added at each sequence position](/assets/images/bert-source-figure-2-input-embeddings.png)
*Fig 2: Segment identity switches from A to B after the first separator, while position indices continue across the packed sequence. The three embedding types are added, so representation width stays unchanged. | source: [BERT, Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805)*

The segment row answers which span a token belongs to; the position row answers where it appears in the complete input. Neither supplies an attention barrier between the spans. Their tokens can interact, which lets a question condition passage representations before an answer-span head scores start and end positions.

### The original ablation supports NSP within this recipe

Next-sentence prediction uses an actual following span for half the examples and a randomly sampled span from another document for the other half. The `[CLS]` representation supports the binary classification loss. This is a test of whether two spans are adjacent under the sampling procedure, not a general guarantee of discourse understanding or sentence-embedding similarity.

Table 5 compares controlled variants using the BERT-Base architecture and the same data and fine-tuning procedure:

| Pretraining variant | MNLI-m accuracy | QNLI accuracy | SQuAD 1.1 F1 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Masked LM + NSP | 84.4 | 88.4 | 88.5 |
| Masked LM without NSP | 83.9 | 84.9 | 87.9 |
| Left-to-right LM without NSP | 82.1 | 84.3 | 77.8 |

Removing NSP has a task-dependent cost, especially on QNLI. Moving from bidirectional masked modeling to left-to-right modeling produces a much larger SQuAD drop. Adding a newly initialized bidirectional LSTM during fine-tuning recovers some of that loss, to 84.9 F1, but remains below the pretrained bidirectional encoder.

This supports both components within the tested setup. It does not show that every later encoder recipe requires NSP. Conversely, dismissing NSP without discussing the original control would hide evidence the paper actually reports.

### The corruption mixture matters more when features stay frozen

Appendix C.2 compares masking strategies. Replacing every selected target with `[MASK]` gives MNLI fine-tuning accuracy of 84.3, versus 84.2 for the 80/10/10 mixture. The difference is small. For feature-based NER, however, the same change lowers development F1 from 94.9 to 94.0.

That pattern helps explain the pretraining–downstream mismatch. Full fine-tuning can adjust representations to ordinary unmasked inputs; a frozen feature extractor has less opportunity to adapt. The mixture is therefore best understood as a transfer design choice, not an indispensable numerical constant proven optimal for every use.

### Corpus size, training steps, and headline scores need separate labels

The corpus contains about 3.3B **words** from BooksCorpus and English Wikipedia; that is not the number of training tokens consumed. Training repeats over the data for one million steps. Sequence length is 128 for 90% of steps and 512 for the final 10%, limiting the cost of quadratic attention while training the longer positional range.

BERT-Base has 110M parameters; BERT-Large has 340M. The appendix reports four days of pretraining on 16 TPU chips for Base and 64 for Large. A Cloud TPU device count is not interchangeable with the chip count.

The often-cited 93.2 SQuAD 1.1 test F1 uses an ensemble plus additional TriviaQA training. The single Large model's development F1 is 90.9 before that addition. Keeping model size, extra supervised data, ensemble status, and evaluation split attached to each result prevents a stronger system score from becoming an inflated single-checkpoint claim.

## High-Level Takeaways

- Masked-token prediction enables joint use of left and right context throughout the encoder.
- Selected targets, replaced inputs, and ignored loss positions must remain distinct in the implementation.
- Pair segments share attention and continuous positional indices; segment embeddings identify membership without separating computation.
- The original controls support NSP and bidirectionality within their tested recipe, with different effects across tasks.
- Full fine-tuning and frozen feature use respond differently to corruption, and headline results may include extra data or ensembles.
