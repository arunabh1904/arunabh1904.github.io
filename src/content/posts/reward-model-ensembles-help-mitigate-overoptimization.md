## 2023 – Reward Model Ensembles Help Mitigate Overoptimization

**arXiv:** [2310.02743](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02743)

**Conference:** ICLR 2024

## Summary

> This paper asks whether uncertainty across reward models can identify the regions where policy optimization is exploiting a proxy. It trains ensembles and optimizes either the worst predicted reward or an uncertainty-penalized reward instead of trusting a single mean score.

## Core Insights

The paper isolates a familiar failure mode in a controlled way. A policy is optimized against a learned proxy reward, while a synthetic gold reward remains available for evaluation. As the policy moves away from the reference distribution, the proxy can keep improving after the gold score peaks and declines. The proposed fix is to train several proxy reward models and use their disagreement to make optimization conservative where the models extrapolate.

![RLHF pipeline comparing a single proxy reward model with an ensemble used during policy optimization](/assets/images/reward-model-ensembles-help-mitigate-overoptimization-paper-figure.png)
*Fig 1: A schematic of the intervention: keep the ordinary SFT and preference-data stages, then optimize the policy against an ensemble of proxy reward models instead of one proxy. | source: [Reward Model Ensembles, Figure 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02743)*

The ensemble members are trained from synthetic preference data generated by a 7B AlpacaFarm model, with proxy sizes from 7M to 1.3B parameters and usually five members. The study evaluates both best-of-$n$ selection and PPO, and injects 25% label noise into the preference data. That setup makes the hidden gold score observable and lets the authors measure the exact point where proxy optimization becomes harmful; it does not reproduce the full uncertainty of human preference collection. The first diagnostic question is therefore: when does the proxy's apparent progress separate from the gold objective?

![Figure 2 from Reward Model Ensembles Help Mitigate Overoptimization](/assets/images/reward-model-ensembles-help-mitigate-overoptimization-source-figure-2.webp)
*Fig 2: As optimization moves farther from the initial policy in KL divergence, the proxy score keeps rising after the gold score has begun to fall—the characteristic overoptimization pattern. | source: [Reward Model Ensembles, Figure 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02743)*

Figure 2 gives the diagnostic that the method is designed to improve: policy KL is the horizontal measure of drift, while gold and proxy rewards separate after the proxy begins rewarding artifacts. A single reward model can therefore report progress precisely when the policy has crossed the useful frontier. The ensemble objectives use either the worst member or an uncertainty penalty to suppress this high-disagreement region. The next question is whether that conservatism preserves gold reward as sampling or policy optimization pushes farther from the reference.

![Figure 3 from Reward Model Ensembles Help Mitigate Overoptimization](/assets/images/reward-model-ensembles-help-mitigate-overoptimization-source-figure-3.webp)
*Fig 3: In best-of-$n$ sampling, ensemble objectives sustain higher gold reward than a single reward model as policy KL grows, while the proxy reward continues upward and exposes overoptimization. | source: [Reward Model Ensembles, Figure 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02743)*

The best-of-$n$ curves show the practical payoff. Under noiseless labels, ensembles improve the gold reward by up to about 30%; with 25% label noise, the reported improvement reaches up to roughly 75% relative to the single-model baseline. For PPO, a single reward model needs a larger KL penalty to avoid overoptimization, while worst-case and uncertainty-weighted ensembles avoid it with a small penalty around 0.01 in the studied runs. The gain comes from using disagreement as a stop signal, not from making the mean proxy more accurate everywhere.

| Objective | Behavior |
| --- | --- |
| Single reward model | Cheap, but easy to exploit outside its labeled support |
| Worst-case ensemble | Conservative where any member predicts low reward |
| Uncertainty-weighted ensemble | Trades predicted reward against disagreement |

### Disagreement is useful only when it tracks extrapolation

The evidence supports ensembles as a way to detect proxy exploitation in this synthetic setting, with a clear cost in reward-model inference and policy optimization. Shared training data, architecture, or inductive bias can make every member confident in the same wrong direction, so random seeds are not a complete uncertainty model. A strong follow-up should vary the label source or representation and test whether ensemble disagreement predicts held-out human or gold-reward failures while the proxy score rises. If it does not, the extra critics provide a smoother proxy rather than a reliable warning.

## High-Level Takeaways

- Reward-model ensembles turn disagreement into a conservative signal for the part of policy space where proxy optimization can leave the gold objective behind.
- The paper's 7M–1.3B proxy sweep, best-of-$n$/PPO comparison, and 25% label-noise setup make overoptimization measurable, but the gold reward remains synthetic.
- Best-of-$n$ gains are about 30% without label noise and up to roughly 75% with the reported 25% noise; PPO benefits from ensembles plus a small KL penalty in the studied runs.
- Ensemble members can share blind spots, so disagreement is evidence about extrapolation only when it correlates with independently measured failures.
