## 2017 – Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms (PPO)

**arXiv:** [1707.06347](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)

**Code:** [openai/baselines](https://github.com/openai/baselines), [OpenAI Spinning Up](https://spinningup.openai.com/en/latest/algorithms/ppo.html)

## Summary

> PPO asks how far an on-policy actor can reuse a rollout before the data become misleading. Vanilla policy gradients take one noisy step. TRPO constrains the update with a second-order KL trust region. PPO keeps ordinary first-order optimization and makes overly large probability-ratio changes stop improving the surrogate objective.

## Core Insights

### Keep useful gradients without rewarding an unlimited update

![PPO's clipped surrogate flattens reward-improving probability-ratio changes outside the epsilon interval](/assets/images/proximal-policy-optimization-ppo-paper-figure.png)
*Fig 1: For positive and negative advantages, clipping removes the incentive to move the action probability beyond the trusted band in the apparently helpful direction. It does not impose a hard KL constraint. | source: [PPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)*

With ratio $r_t(\theta)=\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)$ and estimated advantage $\hat A_t$, PPO maximizes

$$
L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}_t\left[
\min\left(
r_t(\theta)\hat A_t,
\operatorname{clip}(r_t(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t
\right)
\right].
$$

The minimum creates a pessimistic lower surrogate. If an action has positive advantage, increasing its probability above $1+\epsilon$ stops helping; if it has negative advantage, decreasing its probability below $1-\epsilon$ stops helping. Movement in the harmful direction is not clipped away. This asymmetry is what makes the objective conservative rather than merely capped.

For example, take an advantage of +2 and a clipping threshold of 0.2. Raising the action's probability ratio from 1 to 1.1 increases its surrogate contribution from 2 to 2.2. At a ratio of 1.5, the contribution is capped at 2.4. If the advantage is −2, reducing the ratio below 0.8 stops improving the contribution beyond −1.6. Increasing that bad action's ratio to 1.5 still gives −3: the harmful direction remains visible to the optimizer.

Read the two source curves with that sign change in mind. The positive-advantage curve flattens on the right; the negative-advantage curve flattens on the left. The red points mark the old policy, where the ratio is one. These are individual sample contributions. Shared parameters and gradients from other samples can still move a ratio beyond its flat region, so the objective does not enforce a hard box around every probability or a hard bound on whole-policy KL divergence.

### Reuse a rollout while keeping its reference policy fixed

The full algorithm alternates rollout collection with several minibatch epochs over those same samples, usually combining the clipped actor loss with a value-function loss and entropy bonus. Generalized Advantage Estimation supplies lower-variance advantages. PPO is still on-policy: after several epochs, it must discard the batch and sample from the updated policy.

The old action log-probabilities and advantage estimates stay fixed during those minibatch epochs. Recomputing the denominator from the updating policy would keep resetting the ratio toward one and erase the reference that makes the clipping meaningful. With a shared actor–critic network, value-function gradients can also change the policy representation, another reason clipping alone is not a complete stability guarantee.

The paper's small MuJoCo benchmark uses a 2,048-step horizon, ten optimization epochs, and minibatches of 64. The Atari recipe uses eight actors, 128 steps each, and three epochs, with an annealed clipping threshold. These are different reuse regimes; the familiar scalar epsilon does not describe the whole update budget.

### The ablation favors clipping, with a specific aggregation rule

The surrogate comparison runs seven MuJoCo tasks for one million timesteps each, with three seeds per task. Scores are normalized separately so a random policy is zero and the best result is one, then averaged over 21 runs.

| Surrogate setting | Average normalized score |
| --- | ---: |
| No clipping or KL penalty | −0.39 |
| Clipping, epsilon 0.1 | 0.76 |
| Clipping, epsilon 0.2 | 0.82 |
| Clipping, epsilon 0.3 | 0.70 |
| Adaptive KL, target 0.01 | 0.74 |
| Fixed KL penalty, coefficient 3 | 0.72 |

Clipping at 0.2 is strongest in this sweep, but the negative unregularized aggregate is driven by a particularly bad HalfCheetah result. It should not be read as every unregularized run falling below random. The small-task policies also use separate actor and value networks and no entropy bonus, so the general combined-loss formula is not the exact setup for every experiment.

The Atari comparison exposes a different distinction. Across 49 games, PPO wins 30 under reward averaged over the whole training period, while ACER wins 18. Using only the last 100 episodes, ACER wins 28 and PPO 19, with one tie. PPO's advantage in learning speed does not become an across-the-board advantage in final performance.

### A compact actor loss still needs a complete training loop

The paper's lasting contribution is operational. One can implement the core actor loss compactly and optimize it with Adam:

```python
def ppo_clip_loss(policy, old_logp, obs, act, adv, clip_eps=0.2):
    logp = policy.log_prob(obs, act)
    ratio = torch.exp(logp - old_logp)
    unclipped = ratio * adv
    clipped = torch.clamp(ratio, 1 - clip_eps, 1 + clip_eps) * adv
    return -torch.mean(torch.min(unclipped, clipped))
```

In this excerpt, `old_logp` and `adv` are fixed, detached rollout quantities; the helper implements only the actor surrogate. Rollout collection, value fitting, entropy regularization, and monitoring are separate parts of the algorithm. The playground makes the update boundary concrete: vary the old and new log-probabilities, advantage sign, and clipping threshold to see which direction becomes flat.

## High-Level Takeaways

- PPO removes the incentive for an excessive apparently helpful probability change while retaining gradients for harmful movement. The advantage sign determines which side becomes flat.
- Multiple minibatch epochs reuse fresh rollout data against a fixed old-policy reference; rollout length and epoch count matter alongside the clipping threshold.
- The source ablation favors clipping on its normalized small-control benchmark. Atari results distinguish faster learning from stronger final scores.
- Clipping is a surrogate heuristic, not a hard KL constraint or a guarantee that shared actor–critic updates remain small.
- [GRPO](/paper%20shorts/2024/02/05/deepseekmath-group-relative-policy-optimization-grpo.html) retains probability ratios and clipping while using same-prompt group rewards in place of a learned value baseline. [DPO](/paper%20shorts/2023/05/01/direct-preference-optimization-dpo.html) instead learns from offline preference pairs without online rollout advantages.
